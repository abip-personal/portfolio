## Context

Adopting AI tooling in an engineering team is usually reported as a licence count. That tells you nothing. The question I cared about was whether it changed what the team shipped and how often the team broke things — and whether it held up over a year rather than a fortnight.

I co-led that adoption at Creoate across all 12 engineers in the department, and tracked it against delivery metrics we were already measuring.

## What I built

Adoption, not tooling. The work was embedding these tools into the workflow people already had, rather than adding a step beside it: where they fit in code review, in writing tests, in working through unfamiliar parts of the codebase.

The tooling was the smaller half of the job. Alongside Claude as the hosted assistant, I leaned on Atlassian Rovo inside Jira and coding agents like Devin, and we worked the BMAD method as the structure for how AI-assisted coding actually happens on a task. Most of the real work was organisational: defining the way we work, standardising the processes inside the team around those tools, and then defining the KPIs we'd monitor and holding the team to them.

## Results

Over roughly a year, measured against the same metrics before and after:

- All 12 engineers in the department, not a pilot group.
- Throughput nearly doubled, from ~3.5 to ~6.8 story points per sprint day.
- Change failure rate fell from ~60% to under 15%.
- Median cycle time dropped from ~5 days to ~1.5 days.

The change failure rate is the number I find most worth stating. The common objection to AI-assisted engineering is that it buys throughput by shipping more defects. Here throughput went up and failure rate went down by a factor of four over the same period, which is the opposite of that prediction.
